Imagine hearing the same story throughout your life: a hero kills a monster and completes a great deed.

Then someone tells it from another direction. What was the monster like as a child? Could he feel lonely or fall in love? What if the person he loved was the hero himself?

Anne Carson’s *Autobiography of Red* offers an entrance into this change. An ancient myth becomes a modern boy’s story of growing up and heartbreak. The monster acquires his own gaze, memories and pain.

The **2026 Nobel Prize in Literature recognizes Canadian writer Anne Carson**. The official Nobel Prize account describes an inventive body of work that engages playfully with classical tradition and creates new forms for contemporary literature. [Official announcement](https://bsky.app/profile/nobelprize.org/post/3mxecy4iy3c2j)

“Classical tradition” and “new forms” can sound abstract. A few specific books make those ideas easier to see.

## Who she is: ancient Greek and modern inner lives

Carson is a poet, essayist, translator and classicist. Princeton’s Classics department lists publications spanning criticism, verse fiction and translation: *Eros the Bittersweet* (1986), *Autobiography of Red* (1998), and *If Not, Winter: Fragments of Sappho* (2002). [Princeton introduction](https://classics.princeton.edu/department/news/anne-carson-deliver-fagles-lecture)

These overlapping practices help explain her writing. She cares about the precise meaning of an ancient word and about what that word might reveal about desire, loss or memory today.

Classical literature becomes a tool for thinking. Ancient experience and modern life remain different, but they can illuminate one another. A Greek myth supplies figures and conflict; a contemporary love story gives those figures another life.

This also explains why her books can resist classification. Fiction, poetry, scholarship and translation usually occupy different shelves. A Carson book may bring several of them together.

## Autobiography of Red: a monster with an inner life

The subtitle, *A Novel in Verse*, describes a book with characters, growth and love, written through poetry.

Geryon is a winged red monster who grows up in a modern world. He experiences harm within his family, finds comfort in photography and falls in love with a young Herakles. The relationship brings heartbreak. [Publisher’s description](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/24642/autobiography-of-red-by-anne-carson/9780375701290/)

In the ancient story, Geryon appears chiefly as an object of the hero’s action. Carson places his inner life at the centre, changing how a reader encounters his fate.

My interpretation is that a character labelled only “monster” is easy to dismiss. Once we understand his fears, desires and perceptions, the label itself becomes a question. Who gets to define him? How does he inhabit the body other people call monstrous?

His redness and wings preserve the myth’s strangeness. His difference remains. The reader approaches a body unlike their own and recognizes loneliness and longing within it.

### Why write a novel in verse?

A novel can explain how a relationship develops. Poetry can also make a moment linger.

Consider two sentences I have invented for illustration:

> He waited all night and received no reply.

And:

> Dawn arrived. The screen still held last night’s colour.

The first gives the event clearly. The second leaves information unstated and places waiting in a visible object. The screen remains the same while time moves on; disappointment emerges between them.

**These are illustrative sentences written for this article, not quotations or translations of Carson.** They show how imagery, pauses and omission can change narrative speed. A reader moves through events but also stops inside a feeling.

When reading *Autobiography of Red*, ask both what happens and how the passage makes the character’s situation felt. An image does not need an immediate, fixed decoding.

## Eros the Bittersweet: pleasure and pain within desire

*Eros the Bittersweet* examines desire in ancient Greek literature and philosophy, beginning with Sappho’s description of its contradictory sweetness and bitterness. [Publisher’s description](https://books.google.com/books/about/Eros_the_Bittersweet.html?id=itGREAAAQBAJ)

Start with an ordinary situation. You like someone. Their message brings happiness; their silence brings anxiety. Sweetness and bitterness can coexist within the same waiting.

One useful way into the question is distance. Desire reaches toward a person or an intimacy, while a gap remains between what is wanted and what is present.

That gap makes anticipation possible. It also makes uncertainty possible: you want closeness but cannot determine the other person’s response.

This everyday example is an entrance into the book’s concerns, not a claim that every kind of love follows one structure. Carson brings intimate questions into close attention to ancient language and texts. Scholarship can reach a private experience: why can the same attachment attract and hurt us?

For me, the book suggests a way of reading old literature. We can study how ancient writers organize language while using their words to notice our own experience more precisely.

## Nox: why mourning takes the form of a folded book

*Nox*, Latin for “night,” grew from Carson’s mourning for her brother. Its publisher describes a reproduction of a handmade book, incorporating letters, family photographs, collage and sketches. It also works through her translation of Catullus’s poem 101, itself a poem mourning a brother. [New Directions description](https://www.ndbooks.com/book/nox/)

The physical book folds like an accordion and comes in a box.

That matters because a book’s shape changes how we encounter it. Opening, unfolding and returning become part of reading.

Remembering someone rarely produces a complete chronological account. A photograph leads to a phrase, then to the discovery that whole years remain obscure. Some details are vivid; others never yield an answer.

My reading of this form is that collage and folding bring those interruptions into the encounter. A photograph preserves a visible moment, a letter records contact, and writing tries to explain. Gaps between the materials remain.

Translation adds another relationship. An ancient poet faced his brother’s death; a modern writer approaches his words while approaching her own loss. Their experiences differ, yet an old poem offers somewhere for a later mourner to pause.

The book therefore raises questions about knowledge as well as grief. How much can a person’s surviving materials tell us? How do we remember someone when explanation stays incomplete?

## What does formal invention actually change?

Across these books, form affects how experience reaches the reader.

| Work | Forms it brings together | Reading effect explored here |
|---|---|---|
| *Autobiography of Red* | Mythic adaptation and verse fiction | A life story advances while language makes feelings linger |
| *Eros the Bittersweet* | Classical criticism and lyrical essay | Attention to words and texts connects with private experience |
| *Nox* | Translation, writing, photographs and a folded object | Fragments and gaps in memory become part of reading |

The final column contains this article’s interpretations, not separate judgments attributed to the Nobel committee.

We often imagine form as a container for content. Here the container participates in meaning. A continuous account of mourning and a folded arrangement of photographs, letters and words offer different experiences.

This provides a concrete way to understand the award’s emphasis on new literary forms. Rearranging language, materials and reading gestures can give familiar human experiences another expression.

## Why can she feel difficult to read?

Ancient names, mythological references and shifts between genres can create friction. A reader expecting steady plot development may encounter language that asks them to stop.

It helps to separate kinds of difficulty. An unfamiliar myth can be researched. An uncertain image can remain uncertain for a while. Reading does not require a complete explanation on every page.

A Nobel Prize also creates no obligation to immediately enjoy a writer. Some readers prefer continuous narrative; others enjoy pausing over a sentence, photograph or gap. Understanding Carson’s methods gives a better basis for deciding whether the experience suits you.

## Where to begin

For a character and story, start with *Autobiography of Red*. Follow Geryon’s growth, treating photography, the body and love as threads. Mythological context can enrich the reading without requiring a preliminary course in Greek literature.

For questions about love and desire, try *Eros the Bittersweet*. When an ancient passage feels unfamiliar, identify the question under discussion before returning to its examples. How do closeness, obstacles and expectation enter the scene?

For memory, mourning or book design, explore *Nox*. Its physical structure matters; plain-text excerpts can only convey part of the work.

English titles are retained for searching. Chinese titles in the companion article identify the works rather than specifying an edition or current availability.

## What this award makes me want to ask

I am interested in how Carson lets ancient materials participate in present life.

A monster killed by a hero becomes a boy capable of looking at the world. An old description of desire helps us recognize a modern wait. A poem of mourning enters a sister’s handmade memorial to her brother.

These transformations require choices of perspective and material, alongside an acknowledgment that some things remain unexplained. Ancient words gain new relationships, and present experience gains new expression.

That is a kind of creativity worth reading carefully: experiences we think we already know how to tell may still find another form.
